Abstract
This article makes a case for why ethnomusicologists—not just those with specialised
interests in archives, information management, and intellectual property law—need a
serious conversation about open research. Drawing on examples of ethical, regulatory,
and infrastructure challenges encountered in the UK-based Access Folk project, I suggest
the necessity of dialogue that crosses fault lines about data ownership and control.
Articulating a coherent disciplinary-level stance about what open research means in
ethnomusicology is an essential step toward a more ethical research praxis, positioning us
to confront twenty-first-century technical and regulatory challenges.

Keywords: CARE principles; data management; FAIR principles; open research; risk
management

The Access Folk project supports culture change within England’s folk singing
scene through research that prioritises accessibility, participation, and, ultimately, sustainability. This five-year project responds to a perceived decline in
folk clubs since the height of England’s second folk revival in the 1960s (Butler et
al. 2023; Hield et al. 2026; cf. Hield and Mansfield 2019; MacKinnon [1993] 1994). In
the twenty-first century, participation in folk singing skews to aging, male,
heteronormative, white, middle-class, and well-educated demographics, though,
as our research also shows, local realities are considerably more varied (Kay and
Hield 2024; Kay 2025; Hield et al. 2026). Based at The University of Sheffield
(TUoS), funded by a United Kingdom Research and Innovation (UKRI) Future

The case for open research
At the broadest level, open research4 prioritises making the data and processes
upon which research outputs are constructed transparent, public, and accessible.
Originating in the sciences—disciplines that typically prioritise experimental
research design, large sample sizes, strict anonymisation procedures, and alphanumeric datasets—open research increasingly dominates the priorities of
research institutions, funding bodies, and professional associations in a variety
of national and disciplinary contexts (e.g., ERC n.d.; United Nations n.d.; Zhang
et al. 2023; Chawla 2024; UKRI 2025a). The reasons for this push vary, but typically
include reproducibility, developing extant datasets, transparent use of public
funding, and equalising access to knowledge (Borgman 2012; Kraus and Eberhard
2022; Hemmasi and Brown 2025).
Some of these rationales may seem irrelevant to ethnomusicologists, who are
often more concerned with maintaining relationships than thinking about the
nature of the data produced. Our work, after all, is frequently inductive and based
on long-term engagements with participants who help shape research questions

assist the people they record to protect their own rights to their music, dance,
and performing arts” (1996:88). Little has changed in terms of the core arguments for better data governance in the forty years since he articulated his

alongside regulatory and technological developments (Seeger 2023).

share our data, but the very nature of that data, means that a disproportionate
amount of the knowledge generated through our work remains locked away in
personal archives—or destroyed at the ends of projects, careers, and lives
(cf. Luyk 2015; Post 2017:32). Dwelling in the unique characteristics of each case
means that there is no need to develop a core set of tools and workflows. In effect,
we are always starting from the beginning and building bespoke solutions (e.g.,
Giri 2025). This approach is, quite simply, hard. And time-consuming. And while
best-case scenarios may result in some truly remarkable solutions for both

Open research beyond reproducibility
UNESCO defines open research as “an inclusive construct,” based on “key pillars
[of]: open scientific knowledge, open science infrastructures, science communication, open engagement of societal actors and open dialogue with other knowledge systems” (UNESCO 2021:II.6). But as Adams et al. stress, open research “as it
is operationalised in practice, especially in Global North contexts, fails to align
with UNESCO’s diverse and inclusive vision” (2026:4). Scholarly systems of
incentives, institutional and funder requirements, and official metrics tend to
overemphasise the first of these pillars. The open research that counts prioritises
open access publishing (i.e., making scholarly research outputs like articles and
monographs freely available) and open science infrastructure (i.e., repositories
for sharing datasets) in pursuit of the reproducibility goals that are most
relevant in medical-scientific research practices (2026:4). This version of open
research narrowly focuses on dissemination to other researchers. Noting the
host of practices common in the social sciences and humanities that emphasise
research communication, engagement with societal actors, and dialogue across
knowledge systems, Adams et al. (2026) propose a more flexible inventory of
open research practices that move beyond scholarly publishing and data sharing.5 This is still nascent work, but holds promise for validating and systematising the types of knowledge sharing that are most common in
ethnomusicology.
Though reproducibility is rarely a priority, ethnomusicology has a higher rate
of open access publishing than most other music disciplines (Franke 2021).
Indeed, ethnomusicologists have debated and innovated open access formats,
critically engaging the form and purpose of knowledge dissemination, as well as
notions of authorship (Mueller et al. 2014; Swijghuisen Reigersberg 2019). We
have also tended to take a leadership role in publishing open access translations
of ethnomusicological texts.6 This investment of resources reflects a
disciplinary-level prioritisation of access to knowledge within and beyond the
academy, as well as a valuing of dialogue across cultural and linguistic barriers
(Franke 2021).7 Open access publications, however, are typically interpretive:
reliant on the training, analytical priorities, and cultural perspectives of
researchers. But as Seeger long ago pointed out, “The people from whose music

Publishing—open access, scholarly, or otherwise—is a mechanism of openness, offering potential for methodological innovation and co-production, while
also providing a route to research data. Ethnomusicologists have long advocated
the use of digital and multimedia technologies to enable access to audiovisual

Mueller et al. 2014). Especially since the early 2000s, tucking a CD into the cover
of a monograph or using passwords to access streaming playlists on publisher
websites has become normative.8 Less formal options, such as YouTube links or
content hosted on personal websites, are now commonplace options for sharing
audiovisual examples—with varied risks and often frustrating results, as anyone
who has followed a broken link in a journal article knows (Messarra et al. 2026)!
Digital and online technologies offer gateways to data for non-academic stakeholders, with ethnomusicologists and archivists exploiting the potential of the
digital humanities for widely accessible, online, and curated collections (e.g.,
Landau and Fargion 2012; Diamond and Brodie 2013; Giri 2025). At their best,
these websites offer public access to stably preserved collections with long-term
maintenance plans. Unfortunately, such projects too often end without resourcing for updates, suffer link rot, intellectual property and cultural rights prove
too complex or costly to maintain, and access disappears (Seeger 2023; Western
2019). The results of such efforts offer insight into sustainability and infrastructure challenges, but their ad hoc nature does not equate to open research (Harley
et al. 2010; Tal-Socher and Ziderman 2020).

projects from their inception that support clear understanding of what (and how)
data are produced, as well as how that data should be responsibly accessed in the
future. Open research does not mean putting raw data online or blindly sharing
sensitive, proprietary, or otherwise restricted knowledge. It does mean actively
documenting data management procedures to enable responsible decisionmaking about what constitutes the dataset, along with how and with whom data
might be shared—ideally without relying exclusively on the gatekeeping of
individual researchers.
Many ethnomusicologists first encounter the challenges of sharing data when
projects—or careers—come to a close (e.g., Kraus and Eberhard 2022; Russell
2024). They discover the “tricky and time consuming” nature of “adding metadata and other documentation ‘after the fact’” (Hemmasi and Brown 2025:84;
cf. Diamond et al. 2017; Seeger 1986). Names, dates, locations, and details of what
the data captures may be lost to memory, along with the potential for securing
necessary agreements for data to be legally and ethically shared. Hemmasi and
Brown (2025) report on a long-term ethnographic study of punk and metal music
venues in the Kensington Market area in Toronto, Canada, focusing on how they
implemented data governance procedures. The team-based and longitudinal
approach meant that data processing was not always handled by a single person
and that the researchers who collected the data sometimes moved on before data
were fully processed. As their case study highlights, failure to follow a shared
data management plan created scenarios where data were not just unshareable,
but where the project team could not access collected data in fulfilment of their
research goals.
The potential for effective collaborative working depends on research data
being findable, accessible, interoperable, and re/usable (FAIR)—qualities
defined in the dominant paradigm for designing open research (GoFair n.d.;
Wilkinson et al. 2016):
• Findable: Data should be easy for humans and computers to find and
interpret. Data are richly described with relevant metadata
(i.e., description of what the data is), meta/data are assigned persistent
identifiers, and meta/data are registered or indexed in a searchable
resource.
• Accessible: Once found, people need to understand how to access data. Even
when data itself cannot be made openly available, metadata should detail
storage location, methods of authentication, and terms for authorising
access.
• Interoperable: Data need to be in formats that enable integration with
other data for the purposes of analysis, storage, and processing. Typically,
this means preferring open file formats and formulating metadata according to established standards.

One of the goals of the Norway-based MusicLab project is methodological
innovation in empirical music research, including FAIR research design for
documenting performance events (Jensenius 2021). While concluding that robust
data management goes a long way towards answering calls for greater openness,
Jensenius (2021) notes that music scholarship—or, I would argue, any scholarship that centres ethnographic observation, creative and/or expressive practices, and co-produced knowledge—involves specific challenges to the FAIR
Principles. These are: (1) the multimedia and multimodal nature of data;
(2) privacy and data protection; and (3) intellectual property and cultural rights.
To these challenges, I’m inclined to add a fourth related to the participatory and
co-produced nature of much ethnomusicological data: visibility and access
beyond the “official” structures of research institutions. A notable limitation
of the FAIR Principles is that they prioritise description of data over consideration of the people producing the knowledge or the uses to which it will be put
(GIDA NA). The co-produced and relational nature of ethnomusicological data
creates an ethical prerogative to make data findable and accessible to stakeholders who cannot depend on institutional affiliations to demonstrate their
“legitimate interest” in the data.
Multimedia and multimodal data

One of the challenges of open research design in ethnomusicology is clarity over
the definition of data. Archivist Sean Luyk points out that there is a “notable gap
in the literature regarding humanities research data, and very little about
ethnomusicology, or music at all for that matter” (2015:3). This is a problem
for ethnomusicologists because our data takes so many forms, extending considerably beyond the audiovisual recordings that tend to be the focus of available
literature. Our data include text-based formats that are common across the
social sciences and humanities—like questionnaires, interview transcripts, and
fieldnotes. But it also includes music notation and transcriptions that may
require specialised knowledge, systems, and software to generate, preserve,
and access. The ephemera of performances and participatory cultural activities
are data, and include promotional materials, sacred or ceremonial items, documentation of protocol, or elements of material culture—like instruments and
costumes. Our data include the methods and results of creative processes that
exist in a variety of formats, each with distinct challenges for preservation and
sharing—and which may be best suited to storage within a physical archive or
museum. Our data also comprise sound and video recordings that document
wide-ranging sound and movement practices situated in vastly varied contexts.
Recordings serve a variety of purposes, ranging from documentation of
performance practices and repertoire to supporting ethnographic observation
(or other methodological functions), aiding transmission processes, and surfacing relevant cultural protocols. The range of functions for field recordings is

deliberation and debate” related to the methods, technologies, and ethics of
collecting and disseminating ethnomusicological data has tended to happen “for

reached many ethnomusicologists” (2012:128). This siloing of discussion in
specialist literature misses something important about the nature of ethnomusicological data: its multimodal nature. Commentary instead focuses on recording for documentation—recording that prioritises the creation of archival
objects that are understandable as standalone artefacts—without explicit consideration of how sound recording functions as a research process (cf. Fargion
2009).
Roshanak Kheshti’s (2015) theorisation of the “aural traces” of sound recordists, though directed at the production of world music recordings, is useful for
thinking through this question of recording for documentation versus research,
and what that means in terms of the production and preservation of data.
Kheshti points out that recordings capture more than sound; decision-making
based on the recordist’s priorities also leaves marks, influencing focus, start and
end points, mix, and so on (2015:32). Recordings made “for our own research
purposes” include traces of research methods and goals. They might, for example, offer low-quality sources that support transcription, document a transmission process rather than sonic or visual practice, or support practitioner
reflections on their praxis. Recordings “for research purposes” may document
something quite distinct from music or dance, and meaning may be contingent
on other types of documentation. Data, in other words, that are truly multimodal, methodological, and ill-suited for publication without substantive contextualisation or links to larger datasets. Focusing the debate about data,
preservation, and dissemination within specialist discussions of sound archiving
neglects this multimodal nature, leaving us ill-equipped to address challenges
inherent in the unique nature of our research.

cf. Wakaruk et al. 2025). Definitional clarity is also a problem for data management professionals, whose systems may be ill-equipped to deal with collections
featuring widely varied forms and formats with specific regulatory challenges.
Many libraries and archives divide responsibility for their collections by format,
meaning that contextualising texts, transcripts, or notation may be separated
from audiovisual recordings (e.g., Wittgren 2025). This challenge is compounded
by a reality that not all libraries, archives, and repositories have the infrastructure, knowledge, or mandate to preserve and disseminate all data types. Writing
on the proliferation of born-digital heritage objects—inherently multimodal
objects that require “the existence of increasingly complex and flexible
systems”—Goudarouli et al. point to the increased necessity of “high levels of
technical skill from both curator and researcher” to manage “unique preservation and access challenges” (2026:4). In other words, effective management
solutions for multimodal data require multidisciplinary collaborations.

Privacy and data protection

Much of the tension between open and ethnographic research relates to the
privacy of our interlocutors. Ethnographic research depends on rich contextual
details that are likely to identify research participants, even when direct identifiers are stripped from the data. Such details are essential to the meaning and
significance of the data. Ethnomusicologists take a negotiated approach to
identifying research participants, recognising that the co-produced nature of
our research comes with ethical obligations to respect interlocutors’ wishes
about how they are identified. Some musicians and dancers want to be publicly
recognised for their contributions, while others eschew acknowledgement or
prefer anonymity (Hield et al. 2024). The nature of our research area, too, means
that separating traditional knowledge from originator(s) poses risks to creators
and communities: tracking data provenance is essential to ensuring appropriate
access and usage (Zemp 1996; Guy 2002; Draisey-Collishaw 2025). In short,
research that focuses on expressive practices often requires identification of
practitioners because (1) documenting the practice requires accurate capture of
physical likenesses and/or identifying sonic features, and (2) practitioners need
to be named in the metadata to protect their intellectual property and/or
cultural rights.

subject’),” including “a name, an identification number, location data, an online
identifier or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological,

Intellectual property and cultural rights

Ethnomusicologists have long been aware that recordings made in the field have
the potential to attract the interest of individuals who lack commitment to the
rights of our interlocutors. Materials get taken and repurposed, used without
reference to cultural meaning or protocols, and creators go unacknowledged and
uncompensated for their knowledge and art. Scenarios for the exploitation of
intellectual property and/or the violation of cultural rights are well-documented
(e.g., Zemp 1996; Guy 2002; Pruett 2011), as are the incommensurate nature of
cultural rights and existing intellectual property right regimes (e.g., Weintraub
and Yung 2009; Post 2017; Reed 2019) and unequal impacts of regulation across
traditions and geographies (e.g., Stobart 2017; Born and Eisenberg 2025). More
positively, concurrent with the rise of easy-to-access digital technologies,
archiving projects are demonstrating how ownership, control, and access to
knowledge might be re/negotiated, either through repatriation of extant collections or through community-engaged digital preservation initiatives (e.g.,
Landau and Fargion 2012; Giri 2025; Risk and Tulk 2025). While the issues raised
by these initiatives are relevant to open research, reporting tends to be on
individual projects with bespoke “archive as output” solutions. Little attention is
given to more systemic approaches to data governance and mobilisation, or how
risks and rewards might play out in research that does not prioritise archiving as
a named project output (cf. Seeger 2023).
Why does taking a more systemic approach to rights management matter?
Even under the best of circumstances, rights research is challenging and slow.11
Clearing an entire dataset retrospectively is almost impossible without metadata
documenting provenance and terms of reuse (Diamond et al. 2017). Indeed, lack
of relevant documentation is often a key factor that keeps ethnomusicological
data locked away in private collections. That is because sharing data with
intellectual property rights involves risks—risks to rights holders, risks to the
people and platforms doing the sharing, and risks to (re)users. These legal,
financial, and ethical risks are amplified in digital contexts where sharing is,
to a degree, uncontrolled (and without the statutory and moral safeguards
traditionally provided by brick-and-mortar archives).12 Open data repositories,
in consequence, tend to be risk intolerant: only data that exist in the public
domain or that have clear agreements in place with all rights holders can be
deposited (e.g., UK Data Service 2026a; cf. Hemmasi and Brown 2025; Korn et al.
2026).

Risk aversion—and the desire to protect rights holders—might be reasonable, but digging into what this means in practice reveals the impracticalities. UK
intellectual property law, for example, grants creators automatic and nontransferable moral rights over their creations. This includes the right of attribution: the right to be named as a creator. Original works also hold copyright(s)
that give creators the exclusive right to control the use of their work. While
moral rights do not expire, copyright typically lasts for at least fifty years after
the death of the creator (Berne Convention 1886), though many countries—like
the UK—have extended this to seventy years (UK Intellectual Property Act 2014).
Given the multiplicity of rights holders involved in documentation of creative
practices (e.g., rights holders for a single audio recording potentially include
musicians, composers and lyricists, publishers, and the recordist), it is often
impractical to identify (let alone gain consent from) everyone involved. Indeed,
the increased duration of copyright over the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, when combined with the non-commercial nature of documentation and
high rates of risk aversion, has resulted in what intellectual property rights
scholar Naomi Korn terms “a black hole of 20th [and 21st] century content”
suitable for digital preservation and dissemination (Korn et al. 2026).
While UK Intellectual Property Law includes a “fair dealing” exception that is
relevant for research and preservation,13 applying this exemption requires a
degree of risk tolerance that is lacking in practice. Fair dealing lacks a statutory
definition, meaning that the courts assess it on a case-by-case basis that accounts
for whether usage affects the market for the original work, as well as whether the
amount of the work taken is reasonable and appropriate (Wakaruk et al. 2025).
Updates in 2014 broadened fair dealing exceptions, especially in the areas of
parody, citation, and research, but invoking exceptions in relation to data
preservation tends to depend on the availability of controlled access archives
(Intellectual Property Office 2014). Small and specialist multimedia archives that
more commonly handle these types of data may use a variety of risk management tools (like provenance research and takedown notices) to assess, make
available data, and manage potential rights infringements (Korn 2021; Korn et al.
2026), but such services are unequally available and often underresourced.

also neglects the interests of the people with whom we do research and avoids
surfacing the necessity of disciplinary-level debate about what constitutes
ethical risk-managed approaches to sharing data with complex rights (Bishop
2009). While informed consent processes (written, aural, and/or continuous),

Co-produced knowledge and access

Despite the specific challenges that ethnomusicological research poses to the
FAIR Principles, they are an effective starting point for planning research,
encouraging attention to consistent data description, use of open storage formats, and consideration of how data might legally and ethically be processed into
the future. FAIR also offers standardised language for interdisciplinary communication about research and data management requirements. A fundamental
limitation of the Principles, however, is their narrow focus on the characteristics
of the data. Indeed, with their origins in the sciences and prioritisation of a
digital-first understanding of data, the FAIR Principles do not adequately account
for the ethical obligations inherent in co-produced datasets.
Data produced in conjunction with research institutions typically flows
through researchers and remains in restricted project archives in compliance
with data protection, privacy, and intellectual property regulations. With forethought and planning, researchers can resist this enclosure through contracts
and agreements that ensure partners retain control of their data. These efforts,
however, are constrained by broader structural challenges (Vince-Myers 2025).
Institutional data repositories serve the needs of researchers, with little consideration of other potential users; external partners are thus unable to directly
deposit their data, necessitating dependence on researchers with institutional
affiliations. Moreover, authentication processes that are in place to assess “legitimate interest” may restrict access to researchers with institutional affiliations.

Access Folk and the challenge of openness
My first task on joining the Access Folk team was to realise a framework for
action research initiatives that tested strategies for increasing access to participatory folk singing opportunities within England. Action research is an iterative
methodology that is most frequently deployed in messy, real-world scenarios
where change is sought but too many uncontrolled variables exist to disentangle.
Instead, plans are made to work towards a desired goal. Those plans are acted
upon, and then participants, organisers, and researchers reflect on whether
the desired end was met. This kicks off another iterative cycle, with small
changes that enable progress towards the desired outcome (Lewin 1946; Reason
and Bradbury 2024). Positioned as responses to our earlier research (especially
Bones et al. 2024; Butler et al. 2023), we made available thirteen £1000 Small Project
Grants (SPGs) for individuals and/or organisations interested in running projects
that offered novel approaches to widening participation. By inviting project proposals, we sought to recruit knowledgeable local experts with varied lived experiences to weigh in on what access means to them.15 We envisaged each project as a
case study for a particular context, with feedback about what worked, what
showed potential, and ideas for next steps that could be iterated in an extension
phase or passed on to other organisers to adapt and trial in their respective
contexts. Sharing data about projects with our research partners and the wider
folk singing scene, in other words, was essential. We did not, however, conceptualise SPG projects as including a bespoke archival or digital humanities output.

Practical challenges: Folk singing in England

The SPG projects focused on amateur participation in the folk scene. Labels like
“singaround,” “song share,” “folk club,” “singing session,” “floor spot,” or “chorus
singing” are insider terms describing a variety of communal singing practices that
involve people coming together, often in a pub, around the practice of singing
(Wettermark 2025). Participation includes both professionals and amateurs,
though status and role within the singing activity have more to do with perceived
relationship to “the tradition” than professional standing. While there are clear
leaders in the performance, that role may pass to others as people take turns,
sometimes singing solo, sometimes in groups, and sometimes joining a soloist on
choruses (MacKinnon [1993] 1994; Hield and Crossley 2014; Dunn [1980] 2015;
Gordon et al. 2024).21 Gatherings typically depend on volunteers for organisation,
with those individuals determining the character of the activities they lead
through (often unconscious) decision-making about everything from times and
places for gathering, to the layout of spaces, flow, how participants join in (Hield
2013), behaviour moderation (Hield and Mansfield 2019), the nature of acceptable
contributions (Mansfield 2026; Miller 2026), and marketing.

A plan undone: The limits of our institutional repository

Despite our carefully planned consent and data intake processes, within a year it
became apparent that ORDA was not fit for our purposes. Our first brush with this
reality came in September 2024 when we tried to share a short field recording
from a workshop run by SPG holders Sarah Hinds and Miriam McDonald
(Heartwood Social Farming Centre n.d.). Seeking to improve rural access and
mobilise multiple “folk song adjacent” interest groups, the duo offered workshops that explored intersections between landwork, craft, song, and folkloric
knowledge at Heartwood Social Farming Centre, just outside of
Matlock, Derbyshire (Draisey-Collishaw and Wettermark 2024; cf. Camlin
2024). Sharing this particular field recording via ORDA should have been
straightforward: I was the recordist and the video was framed so that Hinds’s
image and voice were the only identifiable elements. The workshop participants
had, in any case, consented to videorecording, photography, long-term archiving, and reuse—details that I discussed directly with the participants before I
made the recording. Trying to share the recording showed up a first major
structural barrier to using ORDA to store ethnographic data.
The field recording features Hinds using a call-and-response approach to
teach a song chorus while participants harvest willow branches for use in basket
weaving. The chorus is from “Willow,” an original song by Rachel Newton and
Sechou Keita that was released in 2019 on The Lost Words (Spell Songs 2019). The
content of the field recording, in other words, includes third-party copyrights
that require clearance before sharing via ORDA. In this case, identifying the
songwriters was easy, though finding out who owns the publishing rights has
proven complicated—as of February 2026, we still do not have a license to share
the recording. But we also questioned whether we needed a license. Given the
content and quality of the recording, did our usage constitute “fair dealing”?
Rather than high-quality audiovisual documentation of a performance and song,
this lo-fi field recording—made “for our own research purposes”—features a
single line of the song chorus sung repetitively. The recording focuses on the
means and context for participant engagement, illustrating details outlined in
reporting by SPG holders, testimonials by participants, and my own fieldnotes
about how the functional approach to singing supports access for different
populations. The song and performance are incidental and the recording is
unlikely to affect the market value of the original work.
This example exemplifies the nature of multimodal ethnomusicological data:
the recording is only meaningful data when combined with other documentation. While the data infrastructure for ORDA is well-suited to preserve and share
multimodal digital data, the risk-intolerant nature of the repository means that
content under copyright—even when there is an applicable fair dealing exception—cannot be stored or shared. As Wakaruk et al. explain, “Anyone who works
with copyright in higher education knows that the interpretation of related legal
provisions is often case-specific and does not lend itself to bright-line legal tests.

Investigating alternatives and weighing priorities

In early summer 2025, we turned to the UK Data Service—the UK’s largest digital
repository for social science and humanities research—as a solution for our data
sharing challenges. Like ORDA in terms of function, this specialist service offers
controlled access to data collections, a characteristic that we hoped would enable
a risk-managed approach to the rights and provenance issues inherent in our
data. Depositing to this service, however, requires institutional affiliation. Even
more problematically given our prioritisation of data sharing with stakeholders
in the folk scene, institutional affiliation is also required to use controlled access
collections. In the end, asking “open for whom” proved unnecessary. The risks
around consent and anonymity inherent in our data, the UK Data Service
assessed, would require their SecureLab, a service that “provides controlled
access to data that are too detailed, sensitive or confidential to be made available
under the standard End User Licence or Special License” (UK Data Service 2026b).
Unfortunately, this service is only available for use with quantitative datasets,
which ruled us out.
Our discussions with the UK Data Service made one thing very clear: though
most of our data take the form of interview transcriptions, written reflections,
and fieldnotes, even minimal inclusion of audiovisual formats with complex
rights all but requires involvement of a sound archive. More specifically, we
needed specialist knowledge of relevant regulatory issues, as well as the capacity
to take a more risk-managed approach to our dataset. In late autumn 2025, we
turned to the British Library Sound Archive: to our knowledge, the only sound
archive available to us in England with a national remit suitable to the scope of
our project.

Open research in ethnomusicology
The challenges I’ve outlined in this article are complexly entangled and play out
in highly localised ways. Finding solutions cannot depend on lone ethnomusicologists engineering bespoke workarounds to their data management needs—
or, indeed, ignoring core challenges by locking data away in personal archives.
Nor can we depend on institutional research support teams or external repositories and archives to engineer solutions to challenges that they are ill-positioned to
fully comprehend. Their interests and priorities, moreover, may at times exist in
tension with those of music scholars. Structured with anonymous quantitative
datasets in mind, institutional repositories, for example, support academic publishing and enable researchers to meet funder requirements for open datasets.
They are often ill-suited to host ethnographic data that include in/direct identifiers and complex intellectual property rights, or to accession co-produced data
that require interfacing beyond researcher communities. Given the costs and
complexities, there is little motivation to proactively address key challenges for
music scholars. Archives, on the other hand, tend to prioritise potential for
dissemination and community engagement. Capacity and resource limits, however, mean that they are often reluctant to preserve data with limited potential for
reuse because of problems with provenance or regulatory restrictions.

Finding solutions that meet the needs of ethnomusicological research, I would
like to suggest, requires four concerted disciplinary-level actions:
1. Defining ethnomusicological data in terms of recognised data governance principles (e.g., FAIR, CARE, or other) so that we can more effectively
articulate our data management needs to relevant non-ethnomusicologist
stakeholders. This includes identifying the limits of extant systems when it
comes to methodological priorities and disciplinary norms, ethical considerations, data ownership, approaches to risk management, regulatory
compliance, and infrastructure. Arriving at a definition of ethnomusicological data that is useful beyond individual circumstance will require the
input and expertise of ethnomusicologists working in a variety of inter/
national contexts and to vastly varying ends.
2. Multidisciplinary collaborations on systematic approaches to data
management that have potential for application beyond individual projects and research contexts. Bringing together ethnomusicologists with
data management professionals, systems engineers, and legal scholars
offers potential for exploring solutions, like how the structures of open
data repositories might more effectively interface with traditional brickand-mortar archives or how smart contracts might use Creative Commons
Licences and Traditional Knowledge labels to track provenance, enable
access, and protect rights holders. The possibilities for creative solutions
are vast if we bring a clear definition of our data management needs (and
those of the communities we work with) into dialogue with the ingenuity
of multidisciplinary teams.
3. Replicating data management solutions in wide-ranging inter/national
contexts. Testing approaches to data management that are intended for
more systematic application in a variety of research, regulatory, and
cultural contexts offers potential for understanding how systems can be
localised without requiring reinvention. Developing standardised workflows, systems, and infrastructure offers greater potential for supporting
sustainable access to the knowledge that our partners and participants
share.
4. Education on data management, especially how principles of data governance might more consistently be integrated into research design. This
recommendation might involve creating training materials based on
solutions proposed through multidisciplinary collaborations or leveraging
existing publication structures to broaden awareness (and incentivise)
engagement with existing approaches to data sharing.
The challenges of open research are unlikely to go away anytime soon. While
open research involves more than data sharing, robust data governance procedures are nevertheless necessary for sustainable openness. Discussing the
data governance principles that support open research highlights how
disciplinary-level neglect of data management fails our interlocutors and
limits potential for systems reform. We need tough conversations about who
owns and has the right to access knowledge (not just ethically, but also in legal
