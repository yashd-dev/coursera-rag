Abstract
JEV decision models pair a language model backbone with a lightweight scoring head
to select among natural-language options. Direct selection compares candidate answers
within one request, whereas entailment reranking evaluates each candidate in a separate natural language inference (NLI) request over fixed relation options. We examine
whether the readout and adaptation choices used for direct selection remain effective
for cross-request reranking. We combine a mean-span readout with selective backbone
adaptation. Mean-span pooling replaces the option-ending pointer with an average of the
contextualized states inside each NLI option, without adding head parameters. Selective
adaptation retains low-rank updates in attention and gated DeltaNet projections while
removing feed-forward adapters. On MC11, which averages accuracy over eleven reranking configurations from seven sources, selective adaptation improves NeoHorse-1-4B
accuracy over the all-module baseline while cutting trainable parameters by more than
half, and mean-span pooling yields further consistent gains at the same parameter count.
Project page: https://github.com/Mei-TN/Jev-reranking.

1. Introduction
JEV models turn a natural-language state into a structured decision that software can act on.
Their typed interfaces include Choice, which selects among described options; Noul, which
answers a binary question; and Score, which assigns probabilities to ordered levels [43, 26].
Open implementations such as NeoHorse-Jev and Kev attach a small bilinear scoring head to an
adapted language model backbone [41, 31], encoding the context and options in one forward
pass and using the hidden state at each option’s closing delimiter to produce a distribution over
the candidates.
These models can answer multiple-choice questions either by comparing all candidate
answers within one request (direct selection) or by evaluating each candidate in a separate
natural language inference (NLI) request (entailment reranking). Reranking provides a fixed
scoring interface for externally supplied answers: their number and wording can vary while
the head continues to score entailment, neutral, and contradiction. The question serves as
the premise and each candidate answer enters the instruction as a hypothesis; selection then


=== PAGE 2 ===
compares entailment probabilities across requests. Because the NLI option text is fixed while its
contextualized states vary with the candidate hypothesis, designs suited to comparing answers
within one request may be less effective when candidates are scored independently. We therefore
combine mean-span pooling over the interior option states with selective backbone adaptation,
gathering information from more positions while updating fewer backbone projections.
The two design choices jointly adjust how option representations are extracted and how
they are learned. First, a mean-span readout replaces the option-ending delimiter pointer with
the mean of the contextualized states inside each NLI option. Because the bilinear head is
affine in the option states under a shared decision query, pooling computes an exact average
of pre-softmax token-level scores and distributes the direct scoring gradient across the span
without adding head parameters. Second, selective backbone adaptation retains low-rank (LoRA)
updates in the attention and gated DeltaNet projections that participate in contextual interaction,
removing feed-forward network (FFN) adapters while keeping the pretrained FFN weights in
the forward pass. Because the head and adapters shape the option representations together, we
evaluate both designs under a common one-epoch, lower-learning-rate recipe that avoids the
accuracy loss observed with longer training or higher adapter rates.
Our experiments on NeoHorse-1-4B show that the scoring interface changes the relative
performance of option readouts, while reducing adaptation capacity can improve reranking
accuracy. With checkpoints and questions held fixed on PIQA and SocialIQA, the pointer
leads on average under direct selection, while pooling leads under entailment reranking. On
the eleven-configuration MC11 benchmark, the one-epoch, lower-rate recipe establishes an
all-module pointer reference at 50.28% accuracy. Selective adaptation raises this to 52.49% while
reducing trainable parameters by 55.2% (from 131.2M to 58.8M), and adding mean-span pooling
lifts accuracy further to 53.39% at the same parameter count. Both steps improve MC11 across
all four fold–seed settings. Under all-module adaptation, mean-span pooling (51.00%) also
outperforms a pointer at the final content token (49.11%). The individual design choices also
improve PIQA and CommonsenseQA reranking, whereas the combined model loses accuracy
on the JevBench and Kev direct-decision suites.

2.3. Selective adaptation
We jointly train the decision head and LoRA adapters [18], comparing two scopes: all-module
adaptation covers attention, gated DeltaNet, and feed-forward network (FFN) projections,
whereas selective adaptation (no-FFN) retains adapters only in attention and DeltaNet. Both
configurations keep every pretrained FFN layer in the forward computation. Crossing these
scopes with the pointer and mean-span readouts lets us compare the readout choices under both
forms of adaptation while keeping head size and adapter rank fixed.
At inference, pooling averages hidden states already produced by the backbone before
applying the key projection (Equation 6). Each option therefore requires one projected key,
and reranking continues to use one backbone request per answer. Selective adaptation reduces
the parameters requiring gradients and optimizer states during training, while the pretrained
backbone remains intact.

3. Experimental Setup
Models and training. Our main experiments use NeoHorse-1-4B [30], a post-trained, text-only
repackaging of Qwen3.5-4B whose backbone combines attention and gated DeltaNet layers [49].
The reference recipe trains the decision head and rank-64 adapters jointly for one epoch with
a OneCycle schedule [37], using a peak learning rate of 1.25×10−5 for both parameter groups.
The learning-rate diagnostics also test 2.5×10−5 , and all comparisons evaluate the final weights
of each run. A separate pointer–pooling comparison on Qwen3-4B [48] examines the readout
change with another backbone.
The training mixture combines web decisions and policy tasks with sentiment, topic, intent,
and entailment classification. Public-source labels and model-generated reference answers are
converted to a shared request format while retaining each question’s native options and target
distribution. MC11 reuses the scorer learned from this mixture: each candidate answer becomes
a separate NLI request, and its entailment probability determines its rank among the candidates.
To keep related records together, we assign whole groups to splits before forming eight
training pools, which contain 17,661 records and 24,001 questions. Each fold trains on seven
pools; crossing two folds with two random seeds gives four matched settings. Within a setting,
each configuration is trained afresh with compatible initial tensors, data order, and augmentation
streams matched across the comparison. The learning-rate, scope, and budget studies reuse the
same lower-rate, one-epoch reference pointers, giving their contrasts a common reference within

4

each setting. Appendix A gives the grouping rules, augmentation procedure, and complete
training configuration.
Evaluation. MC11 averages candidate-reranking accuracy across ARC-Easy and ARCChallenge [10]; four- and ten-option GSM8K [11]; GPQA and MMLU with and without
demonstrations [33, 16]; HellaSwag [51]; WinoGrande [35]; and synthetic chess. Each configuration receives equal weight and scores its candidates through Equation 1. The paired
configurations of GSM8K, GPQA, and MMLU reuse their respective question populations,
changing the candidate count or demonstration context.
Candidate construction preserves the supplied answers for multiple-choice tasks. GSM8K
pairs the numerical answer with three or nine generated distractors; synthetic chess pairs one
legal move with three illegal moves. WinoGrande fills the sentence gap, and HellaSwag uses the
candidate ending as the NLI hypothesis.
The additional reranking evaluations use PIQA and CommonsenseQA [6, 39]; fixedcheckpoint PIQA and SocialIQA tests compare both interfaces on the same questions [36].
JevBench equally averages 18 direct-decision families over 231 questions [3]. Kev equally
averages six development/test selections from its decision suite and two transfer suites [31].
Reporting. We report accuracy in percent and paired changes in percentage points (pp). For
each contrast, we first calculate the difference within each of the four settings and then report its
mean and descriptive SE; SEs for absolute accuracy are calculated directly from the four model
scores. These SEs summarize variation across settings that share training pools and evaluation
questions. Appendix D examines sensitivity to source weighting, omission of individual sources,
and repetition of the reference run.

5. Reranking Accuracy and Parameter Efficiency
The core comparisons use the same one-epoch recipe, with peak learning rates of 1.25×10−5 for
both the head and adapters and adapter rank 64. We first examine the combined design, then
use controls on adapter allocation, readout, and training to explain the choices.
5.1. Combining Selective Adaptation and Span Pooling
Combining the two choices gives the highest MC11 accuracy among the four matched configurations, with less than half the trainable parameters of the all-module models (Table 2). Starting
from the all-module pointer at 50.28%, removing FFN adapters raises accuracy to 52.49%, a
paired gain of 2.21 ± 0.66 pp. Mean-span pooling then raises it to 53.39% at the same parameter
count, adding 0.91 ± 0.35 pp. Both steps improve MC11 in every fold–seed setting. Removing
FFN adapters reduces trainable parameters from 131.17M to 58.82M, a 55.2% reduction (Table 3).

5.2. Adapter Scope and Parameter Budget
FFN adapters account for 72.35M trainable parameters, more than half of the all-module adapter
budget. Removing them improves the pointer by 2.21 ± 0.66 pp and the pooling model by
2.39 ± 0.35 pp, showing that the scope change helps with either readout.
A similar parameter budget can instead be distributed across all projections at a lower rank.
The all-module rank-28 pointer uses 58.12M trainable parameters and reaches 52.40%, close
to the no-FFN rank-64 pointer’s 52.49%. Both allocations improve over the larger all-module
reference. The no-FFN allocation uses the smaller budget while retaining rank 64 in attention
and DeltaNet, and combines with pooling to give the strongest configuration in Table 2.
5.3. Option Aggregation and Scoring Head
To examine the role of aggregation, we compare mean-span pooling with a different single-token
position and a larger scoring head. The last-content control reads the final token inside each
option, retaining the same bilinear head and all-module training recipe. It reaches 49.11% MC11,
below both the option-ending pointer and mean-span pooling at 51.00%. Pooling exceeds this
control by 1.89 ± 0.97 pp, supporting aggregation across the span over either tested pointer
position. The largest gains over last-content occur on HellaSwag (8.10 pp) and the two ARC
tasks; Appendix B gives the complete task profile.
Keeping the head simple. Adding a two-layer residual correction to the all-module pointer
increases its trainable parameter count by about 0.13M and yields 50.33% MC11, close to
the reference pointer and its repeated run. Under this recipe, the residual branch provides
little additional benefit, so the final design retains the bilinear head and changes the option
representation supplied to it. Appendix C examines the residual branch through inferenceremoval and training controls.

We also test the same core checkpoints under direct selection to see how the reranking choices
affect their original use (Table 6). The no-FFN pointer raises MC11 while maintaining JevBench

further, with JevBench and Kev accuracies of 73.79% and 78.12%. These are 3.32 and 3.25 pp
below the all-module pointer.

8. Related Work
Studies of specialized JEV decision models report uneven performance across knowledge,
reasoning, and multilingual tasks [24]. Interventions on labels, definitions, and context also
change their behavior without changing model weights [1, 47]. LLM-as-Jev examines joint suffix
scoring without training and fine-tuning with KL anchors [25]. Related work on prompted
language models shows that multiple-choice scores depend on the scoring interface and option
identifiers [34, 53]. We focus on improving the trained JEV model when it is used to compare
candidate answers through separate entailment requests.
Pointer and biaffine architectures score contextual representations with shared projections
[44, 14]. Research on sentence and span representations examines which token states to aggregate
10


=== PAGE 11 ===
[32, 42]; recent work extends these choices to language models used as text encoders [45, 2]. Tang
and Yang [40] compare pooling and attention while holding the embedding model’s data and
backbone fixed. Our comparisons examine option-span readouts trained jointly with adapters,
including whether pooling remains useful under a smaller adapter scope.
LoRA restricts adaptation through low-rank updates to selected projections [18]. Module
selection under distribution shift [22], forgetting during adaptation [5], and learning rates across
adapter factors and ranks [15, 8] provide reasons to examine placement, capacity, and optimization together. Gradual unfreezing [17], head fitting before fine-tuning [20], and parameterefficient head fitting [50] offer other ways to control adaptation. Fine-tuning stability also
depends on initialization, data order, and optimization [13, 29]. Our paired comparisons bring
readout design and adaptation scope together in a typed decision model used for cross-request
entailment reranking.

9. Conclusion
Fixed-checkpoint comparisons show that direct selection and entailment reranking can favor
different option readouts, indicating that design choices for single-request selection need not
transfer unchanged to cross-request reranking. Motivated by this observation, we pair meanspan pooling over each NLI option with selective backbone adaptation restricted to attention and
DeltaNet projections, jointly adjusting how option states are extracted and how the backbone
is updated. Matched comparisons under a tuned one-epoch recipe establish the gains from
both choices, while pointer-position, parameter-budget, and head-capacity controls clarify their
respective contributions.
On NeoHorse-1-4B, selective adaptation improves MC11 from 50.28% to 52.49%, and adding
mean-span pooling reaches 53.39%, with both steps positive in all four fold–seed settings.
Together, these changes improve reranking accuracy by about 3.1 pp while reducing trainable
parameters by 55.2%.

A.3. Serialization, backbone, and optimization
The request format follows NeoHorse-Jev’s option-conditioned implementation of typed
JEV decisions [43, 41]. A record contains a state and one or more questions, each with
an instruction, a type, and option descriptions or ordered levels. The encoder places the
state after <|fim_prefix|>, the instruction after <|fim_middle|>, each option between
<|box_start|> and <|box_end|>, and a final <|fim_suffix|>. Text segments are tokenized separately without added special tokens; delimiter IDs are inserted explicitly, with no
intervening spaces or newlines. The instruction precedes all option tokens. Option isolation is
disabled, allowing an option to attend to earlier options in its request.
Training packs up to 4,096 tokens, with a 2,048-token limit on each state or branch segment;
the inference server permits 8,192 tokens and rejects longer requests. Target distributions index
Choice options, false/true answers, or Score levels, assigning zero mass to missing keys before
normalization. When only an integer label is supplied, it defines a one-hot target. Training
Í
minimizes − 𝑖 𝑡𝑖 log 𝑝𝑖 averaged over questions within a record, with the ordered-score auxiliary
loss disabled.

forward projections gate_proj, up_proj, and down_proj. The no-FFN configuration omits

Parameters are stored in FP32 and computation uses BF16 autocast, gradient checkpointing,

uses the final weights of each run. The two-epoch runs restart from saved initialization with a

Each configuration in Tables 2 and 4 is trained independently from its corresponding saved
initialization and uses its assigned readout throughout training and evaluation.
The pointer and mean-span heads have identical parameter counts: 1,311,232 parameters.

comparisons add 20 models while reusing the four reference pointers; eight two-epoch models

For each named comparison, we first subtract scores within a fold–seed setting and then
average the four differences. The descriptive standard error is the sample standard deviation of
those differences divided by two. All arithmetic uses unrounded values; displayed means may
therefore differ by 0.01 percentage points from the displayed paired difference. Factorial main
effects average over both levels of the other factor, and interactions combine the corresponding
four arms.

A.6. Evidence coverage and reproduction
Model development used the primary evaluation suites and fold-development data. Because the
four reported settings share six training pools and the same evaluation questions, their standard
errors describe variation within this design. The original GPQA few-shot configuration draws
demonstrations from the Diamond evaluation population; Appendix B.4 reports a separate
evaluation with disjoint demonstrations.
Overlap screening found no exact or normalized state matches between the training corpus
and 231 JevBench records or 6,436 Kev record occurrences, using Unicode NFKC normalization,
case folding, and whitespace normalization. Available source identities likewise showed no
overlap. Further screening covered 30,503 reranking source rows, including 285 MMLU demonstration rows, and the 198-row GPQA file. This coverage is limited to the recorded source text
and identities; semantic paraphrases, substrings, pretraining exposure, synthetic chess, and
rendered evaluation requests remain unassessed. The intermediate SetFit Amazon conversion
has not been recovered.
The saved predictions support independent rescoring of 72 checkpoints: the 56 core NeoHorse models, eight Qwen3 models, and eight higher-rate pointer and pooling checkpoints used
in the fixed-interface comparison. Candidate probabilities are stored with answer identities, candidate order, population information, and eligibility rules, allowing predictions and aggregate
scores to be recomputed while preserving repeated questions and duplicate answer texts. Some
additional learning-rate and adapter-scope results are available only as per-model summaries.
Reproduction requires the base weights, training records, evaluation caches, framework
forks, and compatible runtimes specified by the code and configurations. The retained inputs
supported reconstruction of the corpus mixture and fold exports, followed by one separate
pointer/pooling pair from training through scoring on H20. The OpenCompass and RunWeaver
forks are unavailable anonymously, and redistribution permissions for RunWeaver and other
assets remain unresolved. The initial source-generation and tokenizer-based eligibility steps
remain unreplayed, and the tested environment retains a declared Torch dependency conflict.
CUDA determinism was disabled, allowing repeated training runs to diverge despite
matched initial parameters and data streams. Dropout trajectories were not saved for the
experiments that varied the head and adapter rates together. Appendix C examines execution
variation through repeated-training controls. The 56 core NeoHorse models were trained on
individual GPUs of a shared eight-H20 server, using 94.46 GPU hours over the measured training intervals with allocated-memory peaks of 18.32–19.81 GiB. These measurements include
concurrent-job contention and diagnostic capture; evaluation and the additional experiments
fall outside the timing intervals.

B.4. Additional sources and inference execution
Fixed-question requests. The PIQA state is “Goal: [goal]”, followed by a newline and “Which
solution best achieves this goal?”. Direct selection presents both solutions under the instruction
“Select the solution that best achieves the goal.”, whereas reranking evaluates each solution in
a separate NLI request with the hypothesis “The correct solution is: [solution]”. For the same
1,838 questions, these interfaces require one and two requests per question, respectively.
SocialIQA version 1.4 combines the original context, two newlines, and the question as its
state. Direct selection presents the raw A/B/C answer texts under “Select the correct answer to
the question.”; NLI reranking places each raw answer in quotation marks within “Hypothesis:
[raw answer] How does it relate to the premise?”. Answer fragments remain in their original

C.1. Removing the correction from a trained model
For each of the four higher-rate residual models, we compare corrected and pre-correction logits
from the same forward pass, keeping the trained backbone and query/key projections fixed.
The head computes both sets of logits in FP32 without autocast, so the comparison isolates the
correction’s contribution at inference within an already trained model.
Removing the correction changes only four of 185,900 selected answers, with no correct
answer becoming incorrect, and increases mean MC11 by less than 0.001 pp. This mean uses the
same configuration weighting as the main evaluation. The learned bilinear component therefore
recovers virtually all answer selections at these checkpoints.
