More recently, neural networks are used to generate new relations. One example is memory comparison networks (Andrade, Bai, Rajendran, & Watanabe, 2018), which generalize over existing
temporal relations in knowledge graphs, i.e., the typical ordering of events, in order to acquire new
temporal relations. A similar recent work by Bosselut, Rashkin, Sap, Malaviya, Celikyilmaz, and
Choi (2019) trains a transformer on existing relations in order to infer novel relations in knowledge
graphs. When this model was trained on ConceptNet (Speer et al., 2017), human workers rated up to
92.1% of generated relations as accurate, demonstrating the potential effectiveness of this technique
for already large but incomplete knowledge bases. While current efforts are promising, future work
will need to continue to find and improve solutions to handle the long-tail phenomenon observed in
these knowledge resources (Davis & Marcus, 2015).

4. Learning and Inference Approaches
To solve the benchmark tasks described in Section 2, a variety of approaches have been developed.
These range from earlier symbolic and statistical approaches to recent approaches that apply deep
learning and neural networks. This section gives a brief overview of symbolic and early statistical
approaches to the surveyed benchmarks, followed by a more detailed description of representative
neural approaches, which are the current state of the art on all of the benchmarks.
4.1 Symbolic Approaches
Symbolic approaches use logical forms and processes to make inferences. The study of human reasoning through logic has continued through history, from Aristotle’s theories of logic and deductive
reasoning (Aristotle, 1989), to the reasoning and intellect theories of philosophers like Alfarabi,
Avicenna, and Averroes (Davidson, 1992). These works were a foundation for modern mathematical logic, developed in influential works like the formal logic frameworks by Morgan (1847) and
Boole (1854), and the symbolic logic theories by Hilbert and Ackermann (1999) and Lewis, Langford, and Lamprecht (1959). Later work by Selman and Levesque (1990), Poole (1990), and Hobbs
et al. (1993) further developed this methodology. During this evolution, Peirce (1883) proposed the
process of logical abduction, i.e., the process of making a conclusion using a limited set of observations and a minimal number of assumptions, similar to plausible inference as defined by Davis and
Marcus (2015) for language problems. Bayesian networks have also been used for probabilistically
reasoning with logical variables (Dechter, 2013).

for machines by McCarthy (1968), and linguistics, e.g., in the theory of natural logic toward a
semantic representation of language by Lakoff (1970). Fuzzy logic, which maps linguistic descriptions of numeric variables to probability distributions over the numeric variables, was an important
